# Identification + Gradualization Knowledge

Core concepts for Schwartz's second and third breakthrough techniques: building a saleable personality into the product, then arranging claims so the prospect believes them before the strongest claim is stated.

## Overview

Identification works on the prospect's longing for expression and recognition. Gradualization works on the prospect's need to preserve established belief while being led toward a new conclusion. Together they let copy make the product emotionally self-defining and structurally believable.

## Key Concepts

### Identification

**Definition**: The active process of building character roles and achievement roles into a product so the prospect can use the product as a symbol of who he is or wants to be.

Identification adds a second reason for purchase beyond functional satisfaction: the product becomes a method of self-definition.

**Key points**:
- The product satisfies a want and also defines the prospect to the outside world.
- The role value is merchandising value, not engineering value.
- Identification is strongest when product choice is not decided by function or price alone.
- The copywriter discovers which roles the prospect will accept, rejects impossible roles, and dramatizes the accepted role.

### Character Roles

**Definition**: Personal qualities the prospect wants to express, usually stated as adjectives or adjective-like identities.

Examples include "progressive," "chic," "charming," "brilliant," "well-read," "virile," "feminine," "modern," and "up-to-date."

**Key points**:
- Character roles are often implied rather than stated.
- They are hard to test, so the prospect can accept them with little conscious resistance.
- They should be suggested through symbols, situations, and images.
- They supplement product claims by giving the prospect consolation, fantasy, and recognition.

### Achievement Roles

**Definition**: Social, status, position, or class roles the prospect has earned or wants to display.

Examples include "Executive," "Home Owner," "Man-On-His-Way-Up," "Fashion Setter," "Career Woman," and "Good Mother."

**Key points**:
- Achievement roles must be displayed because the achievement itself is often invisible.
- Products become physical symbols of position.
- The prospect constructs a "material personality" from possessions.
- Achievement roles require more realism than character roles because daily life can contradict them.

### Primary Image

**Definition**: The personality or accepted image the product already has in the prospect's mind before the copy begins.

The primary image may be favorable, neutral, or unfavorable. It cannot be contradicted directly; it must be intensified, subordinated, or used as a bridge.

**Key points**:
- Every product already has accepted characteristics.
- Accepted characteristics may come from structure, performance, cost, history, use, or users.
- The copywriter uses the existing image as raw material for broader prestige images.
- If the desired image is not believable, build a bridge from the existing image.

### Gradualization

**Definition**: The art of starting with an accepted statement and building a chain of acceptances so the prospect believes a goal claim he would not have accepted raw.

Gradualization does not supply proof by itself. It determines the order, timing, and architecture that allow proof and promise to be accepted.

**Key points**:
- Belief depends on structure as much as content.
- The strongest claim is not always the best headline.
- A claim gains force from preparation placed before it.
- Each statement should inherit acceptance from what preceded it.

### Readiness to Accept

**Definition**: Schwartz's belief-centered redefinition of awareness: the prospect's willingness to accept the first statement of the ad as true.

The headline must interest the prospect and be immediately believable. If the biggest promise violates current beliefs, begin with a smaller accepted truth and build toward the promise.

## Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Identification | Building role-value and product personality into the product. |
| Character role | A desired personal quality the prospect wants acknowledged. |
| Achievement role | A social or status position the prospect wants displayed. |
| Primary image | The product image already accepted by the market. |
| Bridge-image | An accepted image used to lead toward a more ambitious target image. |
| Material personality | The self-definition created by the products the prospect owns. |
| Gradualization | Sequencing claims to create a stream of acceptances. |
| Goal conclusion | The claim the ad ultimately wants the prospect to accept. |
| Habit of agreement | Repeated yes-reactions that make later claims easier to accept. |

## How It Relates To

- **Mass Desire**: Identification can create additional mass desires by attaching new roles to the product.
- **Intensification**: Identification often intensifies a role through repeated symbols and situations.
- **Awareness Stages**: Gradualization adjusts the first accepted statement to the prospect's readiness to accept.
- **Sophistication Stages**: Tired markets often need stronger role differentiation and more careful belief structure.
- **Proof and Focus**: Redefinition, Mechanization, Concentration, and Camouflage all rely on Gradualization to time their claims.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Myth**: Identification means inventing a glamorous image from nothing.
  **Reality**: The image must grow from what the prospect already believes about the product.

- **Myth**: A product either has prestige or it does not.
  **Reality**: Built-in prestige is rare; most products need prestige built from accepted primary images.

- **Myth**: Gradualization is just adding more proof.
  **Reality**: It is the sequencing of claims so proof and promise arrive when the prospect is prepared.

- **Myth**: The strongest claim should always lead.
  **Reality**: If the prospect will not believe it at first glance, it should be delayed until acceptance has been built.
