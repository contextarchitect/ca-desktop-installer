# Mass Desire Knowledge

Core concepts and foundational understanding for Schwartz's Mass Desire principle.

## Overview

Mass Desire is the force that makes advertising work. Copy does not create desire; it channels hopes, dreams, fears, wants, and needs that already exist in the market and focuses them onto a particular product.

## Key Concepts

### Mass Desire

**Definition**: The public spread of a private want.

A market exists when enough individuals share the same desire, and enough of them can be reached profitably through advertising, manufacturing, and selling.

**Key points**:
- The desire must already exist before the ad is written.
- The market may be small and profitable, or enormous and broadly shared.
- Advertising speaks to individuals, but its economics depend on many individuals sharing the same buying response.

### Channeling Desire

**Definition**: Directing an existing mass desire toward one product as the way to satisfy it.

The copy writer's task is not to manufacture desire, but to detect it, acknowledge it, intensify it, and connect it to a product performance.

**Key points**:
- You cannot afford to educate a market into wanting something from nothing.
- You can profit by making already-existing social, economic, or technological forces work for the product.
- Running against mass desire usually wastes even strong creative and large budgets.

### Amplification Effect

**Definition**: The economic multiplier that appears when advertising exploits an already-existing desire.

Because the desire has been created by forces larger than the advertiser, a small advertising spend can produce sales many times larger than the spend.

**Key points**:
- Amplification only happens when the ad rides existing desire.
- When the ad tries to create the desire, it becomes education, not advertising.
- Education rarely scales economically for a single advertiser.

### The Three Dimensions of Desire

**Definition**: The criteria for choosing the mass desire with the greatest selling power.

| Dimension | Question | Schwartz Contrast |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| Urgency | How intense is the demand to be satisfied now? | Constant arthritic pain vs. a minor headache |
| Staying power | How often does the desire repeat without being satiated? | Raw hunger vs. a craving for gourmet foods |
| Scope | How many people share the desire? | Gas-saving accessory buyers vs. future-repair-prevention buyers |

### Permanent Forces

**Definition**: Enduring sources of mass desire that do not fade.

Schwartz names two major permanent forces: Mass Instinct and mass technological problems.

**Key points**:
- Mass Instinct includes desires such as attractiveness, virility, and health.
- Mass technological problems include recurring frustrations such as bad television reception, corroding automobile mufflers, or slow relief from aspirin.
- The copy challenge is not to find the desire, but to make the product's promise fresh and believable against competitors.

### Forces of Change

**Definition**: Trends and social shifts that create new or newly dominant mass desires.

These include the beginning, fulfillment, and reversal of trends, plus Mass Education through schools, films, television stars, society figures, group pressure, gossip, product pioneers, and the cumulative effect of advertising.

**Key points**:
- The copy writer must sense which built-in product appeal should be stressed now.
- Timing matters: the shift must be statistically significant enough to sell against.
- A desire may rise, peak, reverse, or be displaced by another desire.

### Physical Product vs. Functional Product

**Definition**: Every product is both a physical object and a set of performances.

The physical product is the material thing delivered. The functional product is the product in action: what it does for the prospect.

**Key points**:
- The physical product does not sell by itself.
- The prospect pays for performance, not steel, glass, tobacco, paper, or machinery.
- Physical facts support the performance claim after the headline, but they should not replace the main promise.

### Dominant Performance

**Definition**: The one product performance that best satisfies the dominant mass desire at the moment the ad runs.

Most products contain many possible performances, but an ad can effectively lead with only one.

**Key points**:
- The headline has room for one primary thought.
- Other performances should reinforce and document the main appeal.
- The copy writer must find the one key that fits the lock.

## Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Mass Desire | A private want shared publicly by enough people to create a market. |
| Market | A statistically significant group sharing a desire and able to repay the cost of reaching and selling them. |
| Amplification Effect | The sales multiplier created when advertising directs existing desire instead of creating it. |
| Mass Instinct | Permanent human desires such as attractiveness, virility, and health. |
| Mass Education | Social spread of wants and standards through culture, group pressure, media, and advertising's cumulative effect. |
| Functional product | The product in action: the benefits and performances the prospect buys. |
| Physical product | The tangible object or construction facts that make the performance possible. |
| Dominant performance | The single product action selected to carry the main appeal. |

## How It Relates To

- **Awareness stages**: The headline begins with the product, the desire, or the problem depending on what the prospect already knows.
- **Sophistication stages**: In established desire fields, the copy writer must make the same basic promise fresh, believable, and distinct.
- **Headlines**: The headline embodies the choice of dominant desire and dominant performance.
- **Creative planning**: Market diagnosis begins by inventorying desires before writing copy.
- **Mechanization**: Physical product facts can create a fresh basis for belief when competitors have made the same claim.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Myth**: Great copy can create demand from nothing.
  **Reality**: Copy can only focus and direct desire that already exists.

- **Myth**: A product should lead with every benefit it can claim.
  **Reality**: The first glance admits one thought; one dominant performance must carry the ad.

- **Myth**: Physical facts are the reason people buy.
  **Reality**: Physical facts document the performance people buy.

- **Myth**: A permanent desire is easy because everyone already sees it.
  **Reality**: Permanent desires are crowded; the challenge is fresh appeal and believability.
