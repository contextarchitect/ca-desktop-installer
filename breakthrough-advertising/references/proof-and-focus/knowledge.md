# Proof + Focus Knowledge

Core concepts for Schwartz's fourth through seventh breakthrough techniques: Redefinition, Mechanization, Concentration, and Camouflage.

## Overview

These four techniques build believability after desire and identification have been selected. They remove resistance, explain how the product works, weaken alternate routes to the same desire, and borrow trust from accepted formats and tones.

## Key Concepts

### Redefinition

**Definition**: Giving a new definition to the product, drawback, price, or application so a sales roadblock becomes acceptable or even desirable.

Redefinition says the product is this rather than that. It removes objections before they stop the sale.

**Key points**:
- Use it when the product seems too complicated, too narrow in appeal, or too expensive.
- Turn liabilities into assets when possible.
- Rearrange the prospect's perspective without violating accepted facts.
- It often works beneath conscious awareness by changing the comparison frame.

### Simplification

**Definition**: Redefining a difficult product or process as easy, familiar, and manageable.

Simplification is needed when prospects believe the category is beyond their ability, even if the product truly makes it easy.

### Escalation

**Definition**: Redefining a product as more important, more broadly useful, or tied to a larger desired outcome.

Escalation expands the horizon of reward and shows the product affecting more vital situations than the prospect assumed.

### Price Reduction

**Definition**: Redefining price by changing the comparison standard or justifying the bargain with a reason-why.

The goal is not merely to say the product is worth more, but to make the stated price feel lower against a more powerful reference point.

### Mechanization

**Definition**: Verbal proof that the product does what the copy claims, answering the prospect's question: "How does it work?"

Mechanization is reason-why copy. It may be named, described, or featured depending on awareness, sophistication, and competitive pressure.

**Key points**:
- The mechanism can be inside the ad as proof or in the headline as the main claim.
- Mechanism copy must still sell; it is not dull technical discourse.
- The more unfamiliar or competitive the market, the more mechanism is required.
- Bargains and price cuts also need mechanisms.

### Concentration

**Definition**: Proving other ways of satisfying the prospect's desire are weak, costly, incomplete, or harmful, while proving your product removes that weakness.

Concentration is direct attack in the prospect's service. It cracks loyalty only when it also provides the compensating solution.

**Key points**:
- Do not attack a competitor unless your product solves the weakness you expose.
- Use contrast, documentation, mechanism, and logical sequence.
- It can alternate bad-good comparisons or move from current frustration to new relief.
- It is effective when prospects already use or trust alternate products.

### Camouflage

**Definition**: Borrowing believability from trusted media, formats, phraseology, and moods so the copy does not feel like ordinary advertising.

Camouflage uses stored trust in a publication, correspondence type, news style, or sincere tone to lower skepticism.

**Key points**:
- Match the format of the medium when the medium carries trust.
- Borrow phraseology from news, reports, letters, and other believed communication channels.
- Use understatement or deadly sincerity to escape the hard-sell stereotype.
- The aim is a smooth transition from trusted content into the ad.

## Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Redefinition | Reframing a drawback, product, use, or price to remove resistance. |
| Simplification | Redefining the difficult as easy and familiar. |
| Escalation | Redefining the product as more important or broadly useful. |
| Price reduction | Making price feel lower through a new comparison or reason-why. |
| Mechanization | Explaining how the product delivers the promised result. |
| Reason why | Mechanism copy that proves the promise can happen. |
| Concentration | Destroying alternate routes to the desire while presenting your route. |
| Camouflage | Borrowing conviction from trusted format, phraseology, or mood. |
| Understatement | A restrained style that gains sincerity by avoiding advertising excess. |
| Deadly sincerity | Frankly naming limitations or sacrifice so the benefits are believed more deeply. |

## How It Relates To

- **Gradualization**: All four techniques depend on timing; the claim must arrive after sufficient acceptance.
- **Intensification**: Redefinition and Concentration often intensify the penalties of the old frame before offering the new.
- **Identification**: Camouflage and Redefinition can make a product's personality more acceptable.
- **Headlines**: Mechanism and Concentration often become headline material in sophisticated markets.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Myth**: Redefinition means hiding a flaw.
  **Reality**: It reorganizes accepted facts so the flaw has a different meaning.

- **Myth**: Mechanism copy should sound scientific.
  **Reality**: It should be clear, logical, and loaded with promise.

- **Myth**: Concentration is competitor bashing.
  **Reality**: It is useful only when the prospect sees the attack as protection of his own interest.

- **Myth**: Camouflage is only layout.
  **Reality**: It includes format, phraseology, understatement, and sincerity.
