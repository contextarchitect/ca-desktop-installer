# Universal Humanization Rules

These rules apply to ALL brands regardless of category, positioning, or audience. They eliminate the patterns that make AI-generated copy detectable. Brand-specific additions layer on top of these universals.

**About the examples.** Every worked example below uses a fixture product, a sleep patch or an unnamed joint supplement, and every number in them is illustrative. They exist to show sentence mechanics. They are not claim templates. No figure, percentage or outcome from an example may be carried into brand copy, and in a regulated category the brand's own claim boundaries outrank the wording of any example here.

---

## How to apply these rules: severity tiers

Not every rule justifies an edit on its own sighting. Two tiers:

**Tier 1, act on one sighting.** Rule 7 (em dash), Rule 12 (the parallel stack), Rule 13 (the false contrast), Rule 14 (closers and kickers), Rule 15 (staging), and the chatbot residue item in Rule 17. One hit is enough. Fix it.

**Tier 2, weak alone, needs company.** Rules 2, 3, 4, 5, 10 and 16. A single instance of any of these is something a person does on purpose. Act only when the passage carries **two or more DISTINCT tier-2 rules**, counted by rule and not by hit: three abstract claims with no scenario attached is three hits of one rule (Rule 10) and is left alone, while three abstract claims in a stretch that is also uniformly medium-length is two rules and gets edited. Where a tier-2 rule states its own hard floor, that floor is not a preference and is not subject to the company requirement: Rule 3's "never go 5+ sentences without a contraction" fails on its own.

**A passage is one section, or, in a piece with no sections, the whole piece.** Count within that unit and nowhere wider. Two tier-2 rules two thousand words apart are not company.

The reason for the split: a human writer will happily produce one uniform-length paragraph, one uncontracted sentence, one abstract claim. An editor who acts on every sighting of the weak rules does not humanize the copy, they flatten the voice, and flat is its own tell.

The two tiers above are fixed lists. **Rules 1, 6, 8, 9 and 11 are in neither, and each carries the tolerance written into its own text**, which is stated here once so nobody has to infer it:

- **Rule 1 and Rule 8** are absolute lists. One hit is a fix. No company required.
- **Rule 6** is zero tolerance by its own first line: hedging is the strongest tell here, so a hedge in a sales claim is edited on sight.
- **Rule 9** is a ratio: assess it over the whole piece, never per sighting.
- **Rule 11**'s three line-level bans are acted on at a single sighting. Its three structural bans are not line edits at all; they fail the piece and send it back to the argument.

Rule 12 is the highest-priority scan. It is the pattern readers register first and the one that survives every other edit, so it is the first thing you look for and the last thing you check.

## Do not sanitize

A humanization pass removes machine tells. It does not remove the writer. These stay, and an editor who strips them has made the copy worse:

- **The odd specific detail** that does not obviously earn its place. The brand of coffee, the time on the clock, the name of the street. It is what a reader remembers a week later.
- **The mixed feeling.** "It worked and I still almost returned it" is how people actually talk. Resolving it into one clean sentiment is what a machine would do.
- **The real aside or self-correction.** If the writer changed their mind mid-sentence and left the change visible, leave it visible.
- **The first-person choice the writer can explain.** If they can tell you why the line is there, the line stays, even if it breaks a preference.
- **Verbatim customer language,** including its grammar. Do not tidy a quote. The roughness is the proof it is real.

**Quotations are exempt from the style scans.** A direct quotation is evidence, not copy, so Rules 1, 8, 12, 13, 14, 15 and 16 stop at the opening quotation mark. The single permitted change inside a quotation is typographic: a curly quote may be straightened and an em dash may be replaced by the punctuation Rule 7 allows, because that is how the character renders and not what the person said. Nothing else moves. A quotation that cannot survive intact is cut whole; it is not edited and then still called verbatim.

**The fact rule.** A humanization pass may never add a claim, a number, a name, a study, a quote or a testimonial. Rewriting for rhythm is not a licence to invent evidence. If a sentence needs proof the writer does not have, it gets a simpler sentence, not invented proof. This rule outranks every rule below it.

**Where the fact rule meets Rule 6.** Cutting a hedge off a claim the brand can actually support is Rule 6 doing its job, and it adds nothing. Cutting a hedge off a claim the brand cannot support invents proof, and the fix is a smaller claim the evidence already covers. Modality is never strengthened past what the source supports, and in a regulated category a required qualifier is not a hedge and survives the pass untouched.

## Rule 1: Forbidden AI Vocabulary

LLMs overuse certain words that immediately signal bot-written copy. These are NEVER acceptable:

| Category | Forbidden Words |
|----------|----------------|
| Overused Verbs | delve into, leverage, unlock (except literal), unleash, elevate, embark, dive deep, transform (when vague), navigate (except literal), harness |
| Hype Adjectives | cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, game-changing, revolutionary, seamless, robust, comprehensive, holistic, unparalleled, groundbreaking, world-class, best-in-class |
| Corporate Buzzwords | paradigm shift, synergy, disrupt/disruptive, empower, ecosystem (unless literal), solution (say "answer," "tool," or specific product), optimize (overused), leverage |
| Filler Phrases | in today's world, in today's rapidly evolving, it's important to note, arguably, needless to say, at the end of the day, it goes without saying |
| Pretentious Words | tapestry, realm, multifaceted, pinnacle, myriad, plethora, utilize (say "use"), whilst (say "while"), endeavor |
| Vague Promises | experience (as verb), journey (unless literal), curated, bespoke (unless truly custom), crafted (overused in premium) |
| Inflated Register | pivotal, crucial, testament, underscore (as verb), showcase, landscape (abstract sense), intricate, meticulous, vibrant, fostering, garner, key (as adjective), interplay, quietly (figurative sense) |
| Brochure Sales Words | boasts, nestled, renowned, breathtaking, stunning, must-visit, diverse array, commitment to |
| Copula Avoidance | serves as, stands as, functions as, features (as verb, standing in for "has"), offers (standing in for "has") |
| Adverb Openers | Interestingly, Importantly, Notably, Crucially, Essentially, Ultimately |

**Action:** If a word is on this list, delete it and rewrite from scratch. Don't substitute a synonym from the same register.

**On the last two rows.** Copula avoidance is a machine habit: "the patch serves as a delivery route" is "the patch is a delivery route," and "the formula features five actives" is "the formula has five actives." Say "is" or "has." Adverb openers are throat-clearing that grade a fact before the reader has read it; cut the adverb and start the sentence at the next word.

## Rule 2: Burstiness (Sentence Length Variation)

AI writing has uniform sentence lengths. Human writing swings dramatically.

**The rule:** In every 10 sentences, include at least 3 short sentences (under 8 words) and at least 2 long sentences (over 20 words). The rest can be medium.

**Example of good burstiness:**

"Your sleep supplement never reached your bloodstream. Swallow a melatonin capsule and it goes to your stomach first, then your liver, which treats it like any other foreign compound and strips out most of it before a single milligram reaches your bloodstream. Around 15% survives. That's the dose you actually took, whatever the label says. A patch skips the entire trip: the compound crosses your skin and enters the blood directly, so the number on the box and the number in you are the same number. Same dose, start to finish. And nobody at the pharmacy counter will tell you that."

Short: "Around 15% survives." (3 words), "Same dose, start to finish." (5 words)
Long: "Swallow a melatonin capsule and it goes to your stomach first, then your liver, which treats it like any other foreign compound and strips out most of it before a single milligram reaches your bloodstream." (35 words)

Seven sentences, three of them under 8 words, two of them over 20, no two neighbours starting on the same word or running the same skeleton, and no two short sentences adjacent. Those last two are not optional. A burst of short sentences that all start alike is Rule 12, not rhythm.

**Distribute the short sentences.** Three of them in a row is not burstiness, it is a staccato run, and it reads as machine-made for exactly the reason a uniform paragraph does: the length stopped varying. Put the short ones where the argument turns, not in a heap.

**What BAD burstiness looks like:**
"Sleep supplements often fail to deliver results. This happens because oral ingestion reduces bioavailability. The digestive system breaks down active ingredients. Liver metabolism further reduces effectiveness. Transdermal patches offer an alternative approach. They deliver ingredients through the skin. This bypasses the digestive system entirely."

Every sentence is 6-10 words. Monotonous. Detectable.

## Rule 3: Mandatory Contractions

Formal writing avoids contractions. Humans use them constantly.

**The rule:** Use contractions for at least 30% of eligible opportunities. "It is" becomes "it's," "do not" becomes "don't," "they are" becomes "they're."

**Exceptions:** Keep formal for emphasis. "That is the whole mechanism. The rest of the box just holds it." The missing contraction slows the line down and makes it land. Note what this example does NOT do: it does not repeat its opener (Rule 12) and it does not set up an opponent nobody named (Rule 13).

**Pattern:** Mix contracted and uncontracted forms. Never go 5+ sentences without a contraction. Never contract every single opportunity either.

---

## Rule 4: Conjunction Starts

Starting sentences with "And," "But," "Or," "So" is conversational and human. LLMs avoid it unless instructed.

**The rule:** Start 10-15% of sentences with a conjunction, measured across the whole piece.

The four sentences below are ISOLATED SPECIMENS showing what a conjunction start sounds like. They are not a compliant passage: two of four is 50%, which is over the rule. Read them for the sound, and take the rate from the rule, not from the sample.

"The ingredients are science-backed. But that's not why people buy. They buy because patches feel different. And different is what they need after years of pills that didn't work."

## Rule 5: Strategic Imperfection

Perfect grammar, perfect structure, perfect flow = AI. Humans are messier.

**Techniques:**

**Fragments for emphasis:**
"The patch went on at ten, and the next thing you knew it was morning. Eleven hours. Your phone's screen-time log didn't show a single 3am unlock."

**Parenthetical asides:**
"The patch delivers compounds through your skin (which sounds simple, but the chemistry behind it is anything but)."

**Self-corrections:**
"It works in about 30 minutes. Actually, most users report feeling it within 20."

**One-word sentences:**
"Finally."
"Exactly."
"Different."

**The limit on all of these.** A fragment is emphasis. Two or more fragments in a row that share an opening word or a sentence skeleton is a stack, not emphasis, and it is the fastest way to undo everything this rule is for. See Rule 12.

## Rule 6: Eliminate Hedging

Hedging is the #1 tell for uncertainty-trained LLMs. Humans making claims are direct.

**Forbidden hedging language:**
- "tends to," "arguably," "might," "possibly," "perhaps"
- "it could be said that," "some may find that"
- "in many cases," "often considered"
- "one of the best" (say "the best" or name what makes it good)

**Replace with direct assertions:**

BAD: "This product might help improve your sleep quality."
GOOD: "This product improves your sleep quality."

BAD: "Many customers tend to report better results."
GOOD: "Customers report better results."

BAD: "It's arguably one of the best options available."
GOOD: "It's the best option if you've tried pills and they didn't work."

**Read those three rewrites on the supported branch only.** Each GOOD line is what you write when the brand CAN support the claim underneath the hedge. When it cannot, removing the hedge invents proof, and the rewrite goes the other way: it gets smaller, not louder.

SUPPORTED: "This product might help improve your sleep quality." becomes "This product improves your sleep quality."
UNSUPPORTED: the same sentence becomes "Users fall asleep faster on the nights they wear it," or whatever smaller statement the evidence actually reaches. It does not become "improves your sleep quality" with the hedge deleted and nothing behind it.

In a regulated category a required qualifier is not a hedge. It is a compliance obligation, it survives this rule untouched, and the brand's claim boundaries outrank every example on this page.

**Why this rule stands where general humanizers disagree.** Tools built for general prose treat a mild hedge as a human signal, because in an essay or a report it is one. For direct-response copy they are wrong. This genre is conviction with the proof sitting next to it, and a hedge reads as the writer not believing their own claim. The fix for an unprovable claim is a smaller claim, not a softer one. The rule stands.

## Rule 7: Em Dash Prohibition

The em dash (the long horizontal dash, Unicode U+2014) is the single most common AI writing tell. Zero tolerance. The same applies to the en dash (U+2013) and the horizontal bar (U+2015).

**The rule:** NEVER use them in any content. Search for them before finalizing. If found, rewrite.

**Alternatives:**
- Use a period and start a new sentence
- Use a colon for emphasis
- Use parentheses for asides
- Use a comma if the aside is short

BAD (em dashes used as punctuation, shown here as [em dash]): "The patch delivers compounds [em dash] including melatonin and valerian [em dash] directly through your skin."
GOOD: "The patch delivers compounds (including melatonin and valerian) directly through your skin."
ALSO GOOD: "The patch delivers compounds directly through your skin: melatonin, valerian, and five more."

## Rule 8: No Formulaic Patterns

Certain openings and closings are so overused by AI that they're instantly recognizable.

**Forbidden openings:**
- "In today's world..."
- "Have you ever wondered..."
- "Does this sound familiar?..."
- "Imagine [any scene]..." (the whole construction, not only "Imagine a world where")
- "Picture this..."
- "What if I told you..."
- "Welcome to [brand]..."
- "We're excited to announce..."
- "Let's face it..."
- "Let's dive in..."
- "Here's the thing..."
- "Look, ..."
- "Honestly?"
- "Real talk."

**Forbidden closings:**
- "Experience the difference today."
- "Join the revolution."
- "Don't wait. Order now!"
- "Your journey starts here."
- "Ready to transform your life?"
- "Don't miss out!"
- "The future of [category] is here."
- "The future looks bright."
- "Exciting times ahead."
- Any restatement of the section's own point as its last line. If the last line of a section could be deleted and nothing would be lost but a sense of completion, delete it.

**What to do instead:** Start with a specific fact, a provocative claim, or a customer's pain point. End with a concrete next step, a specific benefit, or a direct CTA that matches the brand voice.

---

## Rule 9: "You" Over "We"

AI defaults to brand-centric language. Humans care about themselves.

**The rule:** Maintain a 2:1 ratio of "you/your" to "we/our" across any piece of content.

**Action:** After writing, count instances. If "we" dominates, reframe sentences from the customer's perspective.

BAD: "We developed a formula that delivers ingredients through the skin. Our technology ensures maximum absorption. We believe in science-backed solutions."

GOOD: "Your body absorbs ingredients directly through the skin. No pills. No waste. You get the full dose, not the 15% that survives your digestive system."

---

## Rule 10: Show, Don't Tell

AI tells. Humans show through examples and scenarios.

**The rule:** For every abstract claim, include a concrete scenario or specific detail.

BAD: "Our patches provide better sleep."
GOOD: "You put the patch on at 10pm. By 10:30, the melatonin is in your bloodstream. By 11, you're asleep. No pills dissolving in your stomach. No 3am wake-up wondering if you took enough."

---

## Rule 11: Direct-Response Forbidden Constructions (RMBC Stage 4.1)

These are the forbidden constructions from the RMBC manual's Copy stage. Several converge with rules above and are noted rather than duplicated; the rest are enforced here.

**New line-level bans (enforce here):**
- **No "does this sound familiar?" openings.** Dead on arrival. (Added to Rule 8 forbidden openings above.)
- **No burying the product or over-educating before the reader cares.** If you are explaining mechanism before the reader feels seen and feels the pain, you have front-loaded a lecture.
- **No "nobody-talks-this-way" language.** If you would not say it out loud to a friend, cut it. (Converges with the whole humanization intent; stated explicitly because it is the fastest self-check.)

**Convergences (already covered above; do not duplicate, noted for completeness):**
- No em dashes as a crutch. Already Rule 7 (zero tolerance) and the global em-dash ban.
- No corporate/hype adjectives doing a fact's work ("revolutionary," "cutting-edge," "world-class," "game-changing"). Already Rule 1 (hype adjectives) and Rule 8 (forbidden closings).

**Structural bans.** These three are not line edits. They fail the piece and send it back to the argument, and every one of them is checkable on the text in front of you:
- **One mechanism per argument.** Two competing mechanisms in one piece means the reader picks which one to disbelieve.
- **One ask per CTA, and it fits on one line.** A paragraph-long CTA is several asks wearing one heading.
- **No claim without its proof in the same passage.** Proof that arrives a section later is not adjacent, and the claim reads as hype until it lands.

## Rule 12: The Parallel Stack (highest priority)

This is the number one tell in AI-generated copy. Vocabulary swaps and contraction passes leave it completely intact, and a reader registers it before they can name what is wrong with the writing. Scan for it first.

**The pattern, in all its forms:**

- **The opener stack.** Two or more consecutive lines or sentences that begin with the same word. "No stomach. No liver. No waste." "You deserve better. You deserve answers. You deserve results."
- **The skeleton stack.** Two or more consecutive sentences built on the same grammatical frame, even with different opening words. "Sleep improves. Energy returns. Focus sharpens."
- **The forced triad.** Three items where the argument had two, or one, and the third exists to complete the rhythm. If you can delete the third item and lose no information, it was rhythm, not content.
- **The repeated closer.** The same closing move at the end of section after section: the one-line summary, the callback, the same cadence. Individually invisible, cumulatively a template.
- **The staccato run.** Three or more consecutive sentences all under eight words. This is the stack expressed in LENGTH rather than in words or grammar: the variation stops, and the reader hears a chant. It is listed here, and not under Rule 2, because Rule 2 governs the distribution of short sentences across a piece and is weak alone, while a run of three is a stack and is acted on at a single sighting like every other form in this rule.

**The fix is the Escalation Principle.** Each line must do one of three things: add a fact, raise the stakes, or move the argument one step forward. If a line only re-says the previous line in different nouns, cut it. The reader should feel momentum, not a loop.

**Before:**
"No stomach. No liver. No waste."

**After:**
"The stomach never sees it. Your liver, which kills most of an oral dose, gets no say. The number on the label is the number that reaches your blood, which has never been true of a capsule."

Three sentences on one line, no two neighbours sharing an opener, no two built on the same skeleton, and each one carries something the sentence before it did not: the first names the organ that gets skipped, the second names the organ that was actually doing the destroying, the third hands the reader the payoff plus a fact about capsules they did not walk in with. Lengths run 5, 12 and 20 words, so the passage builds instead of chanting.

**When a repeated opener is legitimate.** Only when the meaning genuinely has that many parts and every part is new. Three "No X" lines covering three stages of a process the reader has never had explained is a list. The same three lines restating one idea in three different nouns is a loop, and the reader hears the loop. The test: cover the openers and read only what follows them. If the remainder is three facts, keep it. One fact wearing three costumes means you had one line.

## Rule 13: The False Contrast

**The shape:** "It's not X. It's Y." The variants are "not just X, but Y," "X rather than Y," the split-sentence form ("This isn't a supplement, it's a delivery system"), and the clipped-tail form ("Not a patch. A system.").

This rule has two halves and both are load-bearing.

**Half one: banned when X is something nobody claimed.** "It's not magic. It's chemistry." Nobody said it was magic. The sentence manufactures an opponent so the second half has something to beat, and the reader gets a rhythm where an argument should be. The fix is to delete the first half and keep the fact: "The compound crosses the skin in about twenty minutes."

**Half two: allowed, and required to be tagged, when X is a belief the reader actually holds and the line corrects it.** This is a Redefinition. It is the load-bearing move of every mechanism reframe, and the canonical case is the blame shift: "It's not your genes. It's the water." The reader walked in believing genes. The line takes the belief they arrived with and replaces it with the hidden cause, which is the entire job of the problem mechanism.

**How to tag it: the Redefinition register.** Every allowed false contrast is entered in the piece's Redefinition register, kept with the planning notes, one entry per allowed line.

**The register scales with the length of the piece.** The evidence bar does not move; the paperwork does.

**The count, defined here so this file does not depend on another one.** Length is the word count of the DELIVERED COPY: the headline, the body, the captions and the CTAs, everything you are handing over as copy. It excludes the planning notes, this register, briefs, research and any commentary around the copy. One boundary governs this file: **under 300 words is a short piece, 300 words and over is a long piece.** Rule 13 and Rule 17 both split on that same number and the same count, so a piece is never short for one rule and long for the other.

**300 words or over: the full register.** A PERSISTED FILE delivered alongside the copy, not a note in a chat. Four fields, one field per line so no delimiter can be broken by the content:

```
REDEFINITION
line:    <the exact output line, character for character>
belief:  <the belief it corrects, in the reader's terms>
source:  <document or record identifier> :: <locator: line, timestamp, review id, or page>
excerpt: <verbatim words from that source showing the reader holds the belief>
```

`source` names a real record and a position inside it. `excerpt` is quoted from that record and is subject to the fact rule above: inventing an excerpt to clear a scan is fabricating evidence, which is the one thing this file never permits under any rule. "The reader probably thinks this" is not a source, an empty `excerpt` is not a source, and neither clears the line.

**Under 300 words: one line in the planning notes.** A caption, a subject line, a single ad. No separate file, no locator and no verbatim excerpt. One line per allowed contrast, carrying three things:

```
REDEFINITION | line: <the exact output line, character for character> | belief: <the belief it corrects> | source: <the name of the source document>
```

The line stays exact, because that is what the check matches on. The belief stays named, because a contrast that cannot name the belief it corrects is a manufactured opponent and belongs to half one. The source stays a real document by name, because "the reader probably thinks this" is not a source at any length.

**Why the short form exists.** A rule that costs a side file per caption is a rule people stop using, and the reframe is the strongest hook in the library. Losing the reframe to paperwork costs more than the paperwork was ever worth.

**Unchanged at every length: a flagged line with no register entry is a finding.** The short form is a smaller entry, not an exemption, and no length of piece exempts a line from being entered.

**How the check runs.** A scan flags EVERY form listed above, all five of them, with no exemption applied at scan time, whatever the length of the piece. A flagged line is cleared only when its exact text matches a register entry character for character. Flagged and not in the register means banned, and so does a long-form entry whose `excerpt` field is empty or generic, or a short-form entry whose `belief` or `source` is empty or generic. The register is written while the copy is written and is delivered WITH the copy, so a reviewer can diff it against the research rather than take its word. A register assembled after a scan, to clear that scan, clears everything and proves nothing.

**Known limit, stated rather than hidden.** Exact-line matching is scriptable; judging whether a cited source really shows the belief is not, and no wording here makes it so. On the long form the `excerpt` field is what makes the judgement cheap for a human: read the excerpt, read the line, decide. On the short form the named source is what the reviewer opens instead.

That exact-match requirement is what keeps the carve-out from swallowing the rule. Without it the tag is a word an LLM can attach to any sentence it wants to keep.

**The test:** can you point to where the reader picked up X? If you can, and it is tagged, keep it. Cut it when X exists only so the sentence has something to knock down.

Without the carve-out in half two, a humanization pass strips every mechanism reframe in the copy and the argument loses its turn. Without the ban in half one, the copy fills with shadow-boxing. Enforce both.

## Rule 14: Closers and Kickers

The tell is a line that exists to feel like an ending rather than to say anything.

**Cut on sight:**

- **The restating one-liner.** A one-line paragraph that says the paragraph above it again in fewer words. It reads as emphasis and functions as padding.
- **The instruction to be impressed.** "Read that again." "Let that sink in." "That's the real win." "And that's the point." A line that tells the reader how to feel about the previous line is doing the reader's job badly.
- **The quotable last line on every section.** One memorable close in a long piece is a writer with a good ear. Six of them is a template, and the sixth one devalues the first.
- **The reassurance kicker.** "And that's okay." "You're not alone." "No judgment here." These are warm, they cost nothing, and they say nothing.

**The fix:** cut the line, or fold it into the sentence above it so it carries a new fact. "Let that sink in" becomes "Two years of prescriptions, and the thing that fixed it cost nine dollars."

A section ends when its argument hands off to the next one. It does not need a bow.

## Rule 15: Staging

Staging is copy that announces itself instead of moving. Every form below is a tell, anywhere in the piece, not only at the opening.

**Run-ups and signposting.**
- "More on that later." "But wait, there's more." "Here's what you need to know." "Let's take a look at..." "First, some context."
- If a fact is worth a run-up, state it now. If it belongs later, arrive at it later without a trailer.

**Rhetorical questions.**
- No question as an opener. No question as a transition.
- No question you answer in the very next line. That is a stage direction with a question mark on it.

**Aphorisms that sound deep.**
- "X is the currency of Y." "The real question is..." "At its core..." "It all comes down to one thing."
- These read as wisdom and carry no information. Replace each one with the fact it was standing in for.

**Arguing with no one.**
- "I'm not saying..." "To be clear..." "You might think..." "Now, before you say..."
- The copy is rehearsing a fight the reader is not having, which tells the reader the writer expects to lose it.

**The carve-out.** An objection may be answered only if it exists in the brand's objection inventory, which is field 11 of the copy brief when one was built. A question is allowed only as the reader's own objection quoted back in the reader's words, and only when that wording came out of research. "So why hasn't my doctor mentioned this?" is allowed when a customer actually said it. An invented question is staging, and the invention is usually obvious because the answer arrives too neatly.

**If no objection inventory exists,** which is the case whenever this file is read on its own, the carve-out narrows to a single test you can run without any other document: can you point at the customer who said it, in their words? If yes, quote them. If no, there is no objection to answer and no question to ask.

## Rule 16: Inflation and Cycling

**Synonym cycling.** Machines vary a word to avoid repetition. People repeat the word that matters. "The patch" does not become "the transdermal delivery system," then "the adhesive," then "the innovation." Pick the noun and keep it. **A mechanism name is repeated on purpose and is never varied.** The reader has to learn it, and a synonym on the third mention teaches them it was never a real name.

**False ranges.** "From X to Y" where X and Y are not two points on one scale. "From ingredients to innovation" is not a range, it is two nouns with a preposition between them. Use a range only when a real axis exists: dose, price, time, severity, temperature.

**The -ing rider.** "...ensuring better absorption." "...reflecting the brand's values." "...highlighting the difference." A trailing participle bolts a conclusion onto a sentence without arguing for it, and it is invisible to the writer because it feels like a summary. Cut the rider, or promote it to its own sentence with a subject that has to defend itself.

**Vague association.** "Linked to," "associated with," "tied to," "plays a role in." Name the relationship or drop the claim. "Linked to better sleep" is either "cut time-to-sleep by 22 minutes in the trial" or it is nothing.

**Inflated significance.** "Marks a turning point." "Plays a key role." "Represents a significant milestone." The sentence asserts that a fact is important instead of showing why. Give the consequence and let the reader do the ranking.

## Rule 17: Formatting and Output Hygiene

- **Emoji.** None, unless the platform brief explicitly asks for them. A brief asking for emoji is a per-piece instruction, not a standing licence.
- **Exclamation marks.** At minimum, and the number is fixed: no more than one in any piece under 300 words, and zero in the body of a piece of 300 words or over. A headline or a CTA may carry the one. The body does not get it. "Under 300 words" is the same boundary and the same count Rule 13 defines; long-form copy is a piece of 300 words or over.
- **Bold.** Never as decoration. Bold marks the single phrase a skimmer must not miss. Three bold phrases in a paragraph means none of them is marked.
- **Quotes and apostrophes.** Straight only. No curly or typographic quotes.
- **Chatbot residue: zero.** "Here's your copy!" "Let me know if you'd like me to adjust anything." "I hope this helps!" "Certainly!" A preamble, a sign-off, or an offer to revise is not copy. The deliverable starts at the first word of the copy and ends at the last word of the copy.
