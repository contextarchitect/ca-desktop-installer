# Long-Form Static: Named Patterns

The 10 patterns documented here are non-optional structural rules. Each represents a place operators consistently get the format wrong when not codified. They operate INSIDE the section structure documented in `section-structure.md` - structure is the skeleton, these patterns are the muscle.

Each pattern includes: definition, when-to-use, an abstract structural template (NOT verbatim Rosabella copy), and a structural test the writer runs before output.

## 1. The Desperation Frame

**Definition:** A character in extremis makes an unusual decision. The decision produces the discovery that drives the rest of the ad.

**Why it works:** The unusual decision is the legitimacy engine. A reader who would dismiss "I saw an ad on Facebook" accepts "she drove 6 hours to talk to a researcher she'd read about online." The disproportion of the decision to the situation signals that something real is at stake.

**When to use:** Every full long-form. Most medium long-form. The desperation frame is the discovery vehicle; without it, the discovery feels invented.

**Abstract template:**

```
[STARTING CONDITION]
The protagonist is in a state where the standard path has already failed.
Specific: they have tried the obvious solutions, the popular solutions,
even the unusual solutions within the standard frame.

[THE TRIGGER EVENT]
Something specific tips the protagonist into action. A scene, a comment,
a moment of looking in a mirror. Concrete and small, not melodramatic.

[THE UNUSUAL DECISION]
The protagonist does something that costs disproportionate effort:
- Drives a long distance to meet a stranger
- Pays for an hour of a researcher's time just to ask one question
- Spends weeks reading academic papers in a field they don't work in
- Travels to a different country to sit with a specialist
- Stays up at unusual hours doing research that requires real focus

[THE DISCOVERY]
At the end of the unusual decision, the protagonist learns something
specific. Often a name, a phrase, a single fact. The discovery is
narrow and concrete, not a general epiphany.

[THE EMOTIONAL RESPONSE]
Relief, vindication, or anger. The protagonist's reaction validates
the discovery's importance for the reader.
```

**Structural test:** Cover the unusual decision and read the discovery. Does the discovery feel earned? If the discovery would land just as well after "I read an article online," the desperation frame is doing no work and needs to be rewritten.

## 2. The Discovery Story

**Definition:** What the character finds at the end of the desperation arc. The mechanism enters here, organically.

**Why it works:** The reader has watched the protagonist earn the right to find something real. When the discovery arrives, the reader is primed to accept it. This is a markedly different posture from "let me explain how the product works."

**When to use:** Always paired with the desperation frame. Cannot stand alone.

**Abstract template:**

```
[THE TRANSITION]
A connector sentence that hands the discovery from the desperation
arc to the mechanism reveal. Often: "And that's when she said
something I've never heard before." Or: "The paper used a phrase
I had to look up three times before it clicked."

[THE NAMING]
The mechanism gets a name. Plain language, not jargon. If the
mechanism has a clinical name, translate it to common English in
the same sentence.

[THE FIRST EXPLANATION]
One or two sentences explaining what the mechanism does. Surface
level. The full explanation arrives in the mechanism reveal section.

[THE EMOTIONAL HOOK]
The protagonist's reaction to the discovery. Relief, validation,
the feeling of a long-held question finally answered.
```

**Structural test:** If the discovery arrived without the desperation arc, would it feel like a sales claim? If yes, the discovery story isn't doing its job - the desperation frame needs to earn it more thoroughly.

## 3. Identification Before Mechanism

**Definition:** Mirror symptoms, lifestyle, and failed solutions BEFORE introducing the mechanism. The reader must feel seen before they evaluate a claim.

**Why it exists:** Mechanism before identification produces no conversions. The reader has no reason to invest in the mechanism's logic if they don't first believe the writer understands their specific situation. This is one of the most common structural failures in beginner-written long-form static.

**When to use:** Always. There is no exception.

**The structural test:**

Read the body looking for the first explicit mechanism claim - the first sentence that explains how something works, why it works, or what makes it different. Now count: how many words appear before that sentence? In a healthy long-form static, the answer is at least 60% of the way through the body.

If the mechanism appears in the first 30%, the body is structurally inverted. The fix is to add identification before mechanism, not to compress the mechanism.

**Abstract template (the identification block):**

```
[FIRST-PERSON IDENTITY MARKER]
Within the first 50 words: age, role, geographic context, or
defining condition.

[SHARED EXPERIENCE]
A scene the avatar would recognize from their own life. Specific
sensory details. Time of day. Setting. The protagonist's internal
monologue.

[FAILED SOLUTION ECHO]
A reference to solutions the avatar has already tried. Not a list
yet (that's the failed-solution stack section); just an echo that
signals "I have been where you are."

[EMOTIONAL WEIGHT]
Name a feeling the avatar carries silently. Often shame, exhaustion,
or quiet desperation. The protagonist names it for the reader.
```

**Common failure:** Beginners try to "establish credibility" before identification by leading with the protagonist's qualifications. This is the wrong order. Identification first; credibility comes later in the discovery story or authority closure.

## 4. Three-Stage Mechanism Explanation

**Definition:** Mechanism reveal happens in three stages: name, explain with analogy, differentiate with numbers. Depth of each stage depends on awareness and sophistication.

**Why it works:** Each stage activates a different part of the reader's brain. Naming activates pattern-matching ("I've heard of this"). Analogy activates System 1 ("now I can visualize it"). Differentiation activates System 2 ("this isn't just any version of this thing"). All three together create the "aha" cascade.

**When to use:** The mechanism reveal section, always. Every full long-form. Every medium long-form.

**Stage 1 - Name:**

One sentence. The mechanism gets a name in plain language. If the brand has a branded name, use it. If not, name the mechanism by what it does ("the cold extraction process").

**Stage 2 - Explain with analogy:**

The highest-leverage stage. Two to four sentences. The analogy must be:

- Visual (the reader can see it in their head)
- Familiar (the reader has experienced it in daily life)
- Emotionally accurate (the analogy carries the same weight as the problem)
- Implying a fix (the analogy suggests the solution direction)

Examples of analogy types: a clogged drain, a flooded basement, a misfiring engine, a battery that needs the right charger, a key in the wrong lock. The right analogy is the one the avatar's research suggests they would already use.

**Stage 3 - Differentiate with numbers:**

Two to four sentences. What makes THIS version of the mechanism different from cheaper alternatives. Numbers, percentages, durations, certifications. Avoid vague differentiation ("higher quality"). Concrete: "The standard processing kills 60% of the active compound. Cold extraction at under 40°C preserves 92% of it."

**Awareness/sophistication mapping:**

| Awareness | Sophistication | Stage 1 depth | Stage 2 depth | Stage 3 depth |
|-----------|---------------|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| Problem-aware | Low | 1 sentence | 4 sentences | 1 sentence |
| Problem-aware | High | 1 sentence | 3 sentences | 2 sentences |
| Solution-aware | Low | 1 sentence | 3 sentences | 2 sentences |
| Solution-aware | High | 1 sentence | 2 sentences | 4 sentences |
| Product-aware | Any | 0-1 sentences (skip if known) | 1-2 sentences | 3-4 sentences |

**Structural test:** Read each stage in isolation. Stage 1: did you name the mechanism? Stage 2: can you visualize the analogy? Stage 3: do you have at least two specific numbers or named differentiators? If any answer is no, the stage is incomplete.

### Delivery Vehicle: Dialogue with Authority Character

The three-stage explanation above covers *content*. This subsection covers *delivery*. The canonical delivery method for long-format static is dialogue with an authority character, not exposition. Stages 1, 2, and 3 stay exactly as defined; the dialogue framework is how the protagonist receives them.

**Authority character options** (synthetic abstract beats, illustrative only):

- A specialist met during the desperation frame: research scientist, unconventional doctor, foreign practitioner.
- A knowledgeable layperson the protagonist encountered: guesthouse owner who survived the same condition, farmer with unusual longevity, longshore worker who learned a folk fix.
- A family member who became expert through caregiving: parent of a patient, spouse of a sufferer.

**The dialogue framework, five rules:**

1. **Authority character leads.** They state the mechanism, the analogy, the differentiation. They are the source of the explanation; the protagonist learns from them.
2. **Physical gestures break information density.** Every 100-150 words of mechanism content, insert a physical detail (the specialist pulled out a notebook; the farmer pointed at a tree; the doctor sketched a diagram on a napkin). These act as visual breaks and keep the dialogue from reading as a lecture.
3. **Narrator reacts as reader's proxy.** Every 3-4 lines of mechanism content, insert one line of narrator reaction. Surprise, confusion, recognition, skepticism. The narrator is the reader; their reactions cue the reader's emotional response.
4. **Narrator asks questions the reader would ask.** When the mechanism makes a non-obvious claim, the narrator pauses to question it. The authority character answers. This pre-empts the reader's own objections.
5. **Analogies come from the authority character.** The Stage 2 analogy is delivered as a quote from the authority, not as the narrator's explanation. This places the conceptual leap inside the authority's credibility frame, not the protagonist's.

**Pacing rule:** every 3-4 lines of mechanism content, one line of narrator reaction. Sustained without dialogue breaks, the mechanism reveal reads as a lecture and the reader scans-then-bounces. With the dialogue framework, the mechanism reveal reads as a conversation the reader is overhearing.

**Structural test (additional to the Stage 1-3 test above):** Read the mechanism reveal section in isolation. Are there at least three physical-gesture details? Is there at least one narrator question? Is the Stage 2 analogy delivered as a quote from the authority character? If any answer is no, the dialogue framework is incomplete; rewrite the mechanism reveal as dialogue rather than exposition.

## 5. Bridges Between Every Section

**Definition:** Explicit connector sentences at every section transition. No abrupt jumps.

**Why it exists:** Long-form static is not a landing page with visual section breaks. It's a continuous text that the reader scrolls through inside the ad. Without bridges, the reader hits a confusing transition and bounces. Strong bridges keep the reader moving forward without conscious effort.

**When to use:** Every section transition. Without exception.

**Weak transition example:**

```
[end of identification opener]
I had been struggling with this for years.

[start of antecedent story]
My mother dealt with the same thing.
```

The reader has to do the work of connecting these two ideas. Some readers will; many won't.

**Strong transition example:**

```
[end of identification opener]
I had been struggling with this for years.

[bridge sentence]
And honestly, I should have seen it coming.

[start of antecedent story]
My mother dealt with the same thing.
```

The bridge sentence makes the connection explicit. The reader rides the connection forward.

**Bridge patterns:**

- "And honestly, I should have seen it coming."
- "Looking back, the warning signs were there."
- "But here's where it gets strange."
- "I wish I'd known then what I know now."
- "What I didn't realize at the time..."
- "The thing is, by then..."
- "Here's what changed everything."
- "And that's the part nobody told me."

**Structural test:** Cover up the first sentence of any section. Read the last sentence of the previous section, then jump to the second sentence of the section. If you can follow it, the bridge is missing or weak. The first sentence of every section after the opener should be a bridge that earns the section's place.

## 6. Time-Delay Anchoring

**Definition:** Specific timeframes (week 2, day 14, 17 minutes) anchored before any results claim.

**Why it works:** Specific timeframes outperform generic claims for two reasons. First, they make the claim feel verifiable - the reader can imagine their own day 14. Second, they set expectations, which reduces the post-purchase regret that drives refunds.

**When to use:** Every results claim, every transformation reference, every "I noticed" moment.

**Generic (failure mode):**

```
After a while, I started feeling better.
```

**Time-anchored (success):**

```
By the end of week 2, I noticed I wasn't dreading the morning anymore.
```

**Common time anchors:**

- "By day 4..."
- "Three weeks in..."
- "After the first month..."
- "Around the 17-minute mark..."
- "On the morning of day 9..."
- "By the end of week 6..."

**Placement:** Always BEFORE the results sentence, not after. "By day 14, the swelling had gone down" lands harder than "The swelling had gone down by day 14." The time anchor primes the reader to receive the claim as a specific event.

**Structural test:** Search the body for results-related verbs (noticed, felt, saw, started, stopped, realized). Each one should be preceded by a time anchor within the same paragraph. If any appear without a time anchor, fix.

## 7. The Five Core Feelings

**Definition:** Pick one core feeling and ensure every sentence serves it. The five options are vindication, loss aversion, betrayal, desperation, and identity.

**Why it works:** Long-form static needs emotional coherence. Mixing feelings dilutes the impact. A vindication-anchored ad with desperation-anchored sentences mid-body produces a confusing emotional experience. Picking one and serving it creates a sustained emotional pull from hook to PS.

**When to use:** Pick the feeling at the structuring stage, before writing. Document it in the rejection notes if requested.

**The five:**

**Vindication.** "I knew something was wrong, and they kept telling me it was in my head." Best for angles where the avatar has been dismissed by professionals or told their problem isn't real. The protagonist's discovery validates the reader's own intuition.

**Loss aversion.** "I almost waited too long." Best for angles with a closing window: aging, declining health, slipping financial position. The protagonist nearly missed the discovery.

**Betrayal.** "Everything I'd been told was wrong." Best for angles attacking established solutions or industry norms. The protagonist's discovery exposes a systemic gap.

**Desperation.** "I would have tried anything." Best for problem-aware angles where the avatar has been failing for years. The protagonist's unusual decision is born of having nothing left to lose.

**Identity.** "I didn't recognize the person in the mirror anymore." Best for angles tied to who the avatar is or wants to be. The protagonist's discovery returns them to themselves.

**How to pick:**

Read the angle card's emotional trigger and core desire. Map them to the closest of the five feelings. If the angle's trigger is "shame" and the core desire is "to be seen as capable again," the feeling is identity. If the trigger is "fear" and the desire is "to stop the decline," the feeling is loss aversion.

**Structural test:** Pick five sentences at random from the body. Each should clearly serve the chosen feeling. If a sentence is feeling-neutral or serves a different feeling, rewrite or cut.

## 8. Authority Hook Patterns

**Definition:** Four sub-patterns for introducing authority into the body. Each works with different angles.

**Pattern A - Classic (named specialist):**

A specific specialist in a specific role validates the discovery. Synthetic abstract beat: a named specialist (with role and location) is introduced as the first person who explained the mechanism to the protagonist without dismissing the question.

**Pattern B - Doctor's Surprise:**

A medical professional encounters the protagonist's results and is surprised. Synthetic abstract beat: the protagonist's regular doctor reviews chart or labs at a follow-up, double-takes, and asks what the protagonist changed.

**Pattern C - Doctor's Skepticism:**

A medical professional initially dismisses the protagonist, then comes around. Synthetic abstract beat: the protagonist's first conversation with a doctor is met with skepticism; months later the same doctor asks the protagonist for the source on behalf of one of their other patients.

**Pattern D - Study/Research:**

A study, paper, or institutional finding validates the mechanism. Synthetic abstract beat: a review or paper from a credible journal type, tracking a named cohort size over a named duration, surfaces the pattern the protagonist had been living.

**When to use each:**

| Angle's emotional trigger | Best authority pattern |
|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Vindication | Doctor's Skepticism (the medical world catches up) |
| Loss aversion | Classic (a specialist who saw the urgency) |
| Betrayal | Study/Research (institutional proof of the gap) |
| Desperation | Doctor's Surprise (results confirm the unusual decision) |
| Identity | Classic OR Doctor's Surprise (the return to self is witnessed) |

**Structural test:** Identify the authority pattern used. Does it match the angle's emotional trigger per the table above? If not, switch.

## 9. The Three-Lead Rule

**Definition:** Same body, three POV variants. POV options: first-degree sufferer, family member of sufferer, professional with sufferer experience.

**Why it works:** The same angle reaches different segments through different POVs. The first-degree sufferer reaches readers who self-identify with the problem. The family member reaches readers who recognize a loved one. The professional reaches readers who trust expertise. Three variants per angle covers a wider audience than three angles with one variant each.

**When to use:** Every angle. Default. Override only if the user specifies a single POV.

**POV taxonomy:**

**First-degree sufferer.** The protagonist has the problem. First-person voice. The discovery is their own. Best for angles where the avatar is the direct sufferer (chronic conditions, personal goals).

**Family member of sufferer.** The protagonist's family member (spouse, parent, child) has the problem. The protagonist witnesses the suffering and discovers the solution on their behalf. Best for angles where the avatar is a caregiver, or where the avatar has the problem but is in denial about it.

**Professional with sufferer experience.** The protagonist is a nurse, doctor, researcher, or other professional whose work brought them into contact with the problem. Often they then discover they have it themselves. Best for angles requiring high-trust authority within the body.

**How to pick the three:**

Default to one of each: sufferer, family member, professional. If the angle is strongly first-degree (e.g., menopausal symptoms), default to: female sufferer, female sufferer's daughter, female nurse. If the angle is strongly third-party (e.g., a child's condition), default to: parent, grandparent, pediatric professional.

**Structural test:** Read the three variants back to back. They should differ in:

- Identity marker (different ages, roles, life situations)
- Failed-solution stack (different specific solutions tried)
- Discovery moment (different unusual decisions)
- Emotional response (different but compatible reactions)

The mechanism, the differentiation, the risk reversal, and the CTA should remain functionally the same. The body of each variant should NOT be a word-substitution of the others.

## 10. POV Stability

**Definition:** Once a POV is chosen, never break it. The product introduction must come from inside the POV, not from a narrator stepping in.

**Why it exists:** POV breaks are the most common conversion-killing failure in long-form static. The reader has invested in the protagonist's voice; a narrator interruption signals "this is an ad" and breaks the spell. Specifically: a first-person body that suddenly says "and that's why XBrand created this product" has broken POV.

**When to use:** Every variant. Every section.

**Common POV-break failure modes:**

- The product gets introduced by an unnamed narrator ("That's when XBrand realized there was a gap in the market.")
- The body switches from first-person to second-person mid-section without a bridge
- A Stage 3 differentiation block reads like a marketing brochure ("Our proprietary cold-extraction process delivers...")
- The PS is written in a different voice from the body

**Fixes (synthetic abstract beats, illustrative only):**

- Product introduction: from inside the POV, the protagonist describes searching for the cleanest version of the mechanism after learning about it, and naming the brand they found.
- Differentiation: same first-person voice describes a named process (e.g., cold extraction at a specific temperature) with named percentages of active-compound preservation, contrasted against the cheaper versions.
- Authority: first-person frame around the doctor's reaction at a follow-up appointment; the protagonist reports what was said, not the doctor's voice directly.
- PS: same voice as the body. Specific personal callback to an antecedent character or scene.

**Structural test:** Read the body looking for the first sentence that uses third-person voice for the brand or the product. If any exist, rewrite into first-person. The protagonist names the brand; the brand never names itself.

## Pattern Application Summary

When writing a new long-form static:

1. Pick the core feeling (pattern 7) at the structuring stage
2. Pick the authority hook pattern (pattern 8) based on the feeling
3. Apply identification before mechanism (pattern 3) at the body level
4. Use the desperation frame (pattern 1) and discovery story (pattern 2) as the hinge between identification and mechanism
5. Apply three-stage mechanism explanation (pattern 4) at the mechanism reveal
6. Add bridges (pattern 5) at every section transition
7. Add time-delay anchors (pattern 6) before every results claim
8. Maintain POV stability (pattern 10) throughout
9. Generate three POV variants (pattern 9) at the output stage

Run the structural test for each pattern before output. Document any failures in rejection notes.
