# Long-Form Static: Section Structure

The section structure is the architectural backbone of every long-form-static ad. The same arc runs across the full and medium variants; the medium variant compresses each section by roughly 50% but does not remove any. The fake-complaint short-form is a different beast with its own micro-structure.

This file is paired with `named-patterns.md`, which documents the writing rules that operate WITHIN each section. Read both before writing.

## Variant 1: Full Long-Form (2,500+ words)

**When to use:** Default for problem-aware and solution-aware angles. The full arc has runway for identification, root cause framing, mechanism explanation, and durability proof. Best for cold-to-warm traffic where the reader has not been pre-educated by a video.

**Word target:** 2,500 to 3,500 words. Beyond 3,500 the in-feed runtime drops sharply.

**Section table:**

| Section | Word budget | Purpose | Notes |
|---------|-------------|---------|-------|
| Hook + headline (in-feed visible) | 30-50 | Stop scroll, identify viewer | Max 9 words for the first line. Must work pre-"see more" expansion. Identity marker mandatory. |
| Identification opener | 100-200 | Mirror symptoms, lifestyle, failed solutions | First-person POV. Identity marker (age, role) within first 50 words. |
| Antecedent / catalyst story | 400-800 | The "father took medication for 22 years" type backstory | This is the agitation engine. Twist of knife happens here. Specifics are non-optional: name conditions, ages, durations. |
| POV anchor + decision moment | 200-400 | "Fast forward to 3 years ago, I'm 61..." then doctor scene | The reframe moment. Character refuses standard solution. This earns the desperation frame that follows. |
| Failed-solution stack | 200-400 | "I did everything right." Diet, exercise, etc. failed | Specificity required. Name medications, supplements, doctor visits. Generic "I tried everything" is a writing failure. |
| Desperation frame + discovery | 300-500 | 2 AM research, drives 6 hours, talks to a stranger | The unusual decision that produces the discovery. See `named-patterns.md` for the desperation-frame template. |
| Mechanism reveal | 300-500 | Three stages: name, explain with analogy, differentiate with numbers | Analogy mandatory (System 1 activation). Three-stage explanation per `named-patterns.md`. |
| Product introduction | 100-200 | Soft introduction, organic transition | Never break POV. Character introduces the product, not a narrator stepping in. |
| Differentiation block | 200-300 | Sourcing, process, dose, testing | The "vs the cheap alternatives" moment. Specifics: percentages, certifications, country of origin. |
| Results timeline | 200-400 | Week-by-week, day-by-day | Time-delay anchoring throughout. Generic "fast results" is a writing failure. |
| Authority closure | 100-200 | Doctor confirmation scene, official endorsement | Optional. Only if the angle supports it without straining credibility. |
| Durability proof | 100-200 | "8 months later, here's where I am now" | Optional. Strong for solution-aware audiences who have been burned by short-term wins. |
| Mirror moment | 100-200 | "If you're reading this, you see yourself" | The avatar-mirroring closer before the offer. |
| Differentiation vs others (closing) | 200-300 | Heirloom / cold-extract / clinical-dose style USPs | Bullet-style breaks here are OK. Each USP gets one specific, verifiable claim. |
| Risk reversal | 50-100 | 90-day money back, no-questions-asked | Mandatory. Without it, the ad converts at half the rate. |
| Crossroads framing | 100-200 | "One path: keep doing X. Another path: try this" | Optional but strong. The decision-architecture closer. |
| CTA (1) | 30-50 | First link | Mandatory. Phrasing per copywriting-guide. |
| PS | 50-100 | Specific personal detail, callback to story | Mandatory. Proven element of every Rosabella variant. |
| PPS | 50-100 | Second testimonial, urgency, or social proof | Optional. |
| CTA (2) | 30-50 | Second link | Optional. |

**Total target:** 2,500 to 3,500 words. The section table sums to a higher maximum (around 4,090 words at every section maximum simultaneously), but in practice ads should land in the 2,500-3,500 range. If a draft exceeds 3,500 words, audit each section against its budget and compress the over-budget sections rather than cutting any section entirely.

**Section ordering rule:** The order above is non-negotiable for full long-form. Reordering breaks the Yes-Yes-Yes chain. Specifically:

- Identification BEFORE mechanism (always)
- Failed-solution stack BEFORE desperation frame (the failure earns the desperation)
- Mechanism BEFORE product introduction (the reader buys the principle, then the product)
- Authority and durability proof AFTER results timeline (they reinforce, not introduce)
- Risk reversal BEFORE final CTA (lowers friction at the moment of decision)
- PS AFTER all CTAs (the callback hits hardest after the reader has seen the offer)

### Section-Level Writing Notes

**Hook + headline (in-feed visible).** This is the only part the user reads before deciding to expand "see more." 9 words is the practical maximum because longer hooks get cut off in the feed. Identity marker means a word that signals "this is for you" - age, role, geographic context, or condition. Info gap means you promise something specific (a discovery, a number, a turn) without revealing it. Avoid generic opens like "I want to share my story." Open in the middle of an action: a doctor's office, a grocery aisle, a 2 AM scroll session.

**Identification opener.** The avatar's voice is paramount. Pull raw quotes from avatar research and adapt them - don't invent identification copy from a brand-voice generator. If the avatar research says "I avoid mirrors before noon," that line goes here, lightly rewritten in narrative voice. The opener earns the right to keep reading; if the reader doesn't recognize themselves in the first 100 words, they bounce.

**Antecedent / catalyst story.** This is the longest single section in most full long-form ads. It's also where most beginners weaken the structure. Common failures: too much exposition before any specific scene; multiple secondary characters who aren't load-bearing; backstory that doesn't connect to the angle's emotional trigger. The fix: name one person (often a parent or spouse), name one specific event, anchor it in time, and let the consequences ripple forward. The "father took medication for 22 years" type backstory works because it's a specific person, a specific duration, and a specific consequence.

**POV anchor + decision moment.** This is where the protagonist refuses the standard solution. The doctor offers medication; protagonist says no. The specialist recommends surgery; protagonist says wait. This refusal earns the desperation frame that follows. Without a clean refusal scene, the desperation feels arbitrary.

**Failed-solution stack.** Specificity is the entire job. Generic "I tried everything" is a writing failure. Name medications, dosages, durations. Name supplements, brands, monthly costs. Name doctors, specialties, what they said. The reader pattern-matches against their own failed solutions - the more specific your list, the more matches they make.

**Desperation frame + discovery.** See `named-patterns.md` for the full template. Key beats: an unusual decision (drives 6 hours, talks to a stranger at a conference, pays a researcher's hourly rate just to ask one question), the discovery itself (often a name, a phrase, a single fact), and the immediate emotional response (relief, validation, vindication). Avoid making the discovery convenient ("I saw an ad on Facebook"); the desperation frame is doing the legitimacy work.

**Mechanism reveal.** Three stages, in this order: (1) name the mechanism in plain language; (2) explain it with an analogy that the avatar already understands; (3) differentiate it with numbers or specifics that other approaches lack. Stage 2 is the highest-leverage. A bad analogy here kills the whole ad. The analogy should be visual, simple, and emotionally accurate. See `named-patterns.md` for the analogy test.

**Product introduction.** Soft. Organic. From inside the POV. The character introduces the product as the natural next step ("when I learned about X mechanism, I started looking for the cleanest version of it I could find"). Never break POV here. Never let a narrator step in to explain what the product is. The product enters the same way it would in a friend telling a story.

**The 75% rule for product reveal.** In addition to the sequencing rule (Mechanism BEFORE Product Introduction), long-format static observes a quantitative threshold: the product name does NOT appear before approximately 75% of the body. In a 2,800-word full long-form, that is word ~2,100. In a 1,200-word medium long-form, that is word ~900. By the 75% mark, the reader's emotional investment has compounded across identification, antecedent story, failed-solution stack, desperation frame, and mechanism reveal; the product feels like the natural resolution of the story. Earlier introductions feel like sales pitches breaking the spell. This is one of the more frequently violated rules in beginner long-form static; ads that read structurally correct but convert poorly often fail this single check. To verify: search the body for the brand name and any product name. The earliest occurrence should be at or after the 75% mark by word count. If earlier, audit the section preceding the product introduction; typically the mechanism reveal needs to expand or the failed-solution stack needs more specificity. Note this is distinct from the 60% identification-before-mechanism threshold (see `named-patterns.md` pattern 3); 60% is where the mechanism FIRST appears, 75% is where the product name FIRST appears.

**Differentiation block.** This is the "vs the cheap alternatives" moment, but it's not a comparison chart. It's the protagonist explaining why other versions of the mechanism didn't qualify. Specifics: percentages, certifications, sourcing, processing methods, dosage. If the brand has a USP catalog, pull from it. If not, the differentiation block is thin and the ad will need a stronger close.

**Results timeline.** Every results claim needs a time anchor. "Week 2." "Day 14." "After 17 minutes." Generic claims ("I started feeling better") are a writing failure. The time anchor does two jobs: it makes the claim feel specific and verifiable, and it sets the reader's expectations for their own timeline.

**Authority closure.** Optional. Use only if the angle supports it. Common patterns: a doctor confirms the discovery in a follow-up appointment; a study or paper validates the mechanism; an industry insider acknowledges the gap. Avoid stretching to add authority where it doesn't fit; weak authority closure undermines the rest of the body.

**Durability proof.** Optional. Use for solution-aware audiences who have been burned by short-term wins. The pattern: "8 months later, here's where I am." Specific. Time-anchored. Optional photographs or measurements referenced. The job is to neutralize the "but does it last?" objection before the offer.

**Mirror moment.** "If you're reading this, you see yourself." The avatar-mirroring closer. This is the section that converts identification into action. Compress to 100-200 words. Address the reader directly. Use second person here, even if the body has been first person.

**Differentiation vs others (closing).** The structured USP block. Bullet-style breaks are OK here even though most of the body avoids them. Each bullet: one specific, verifiable claim. Examples: "Cold-extracted at under 40°C." "Tested at three independent labs." "Sourced from the original Mediterranean grove." The bullets do logical-validation work after the emotional close.

**Risk reversal.** Mandatory. Without it, the ad converts at half the rate of one with it. Common patterns: 90-day money back; first-month free; refund the day after results, no questions asked. Specifics matter; "satisfaction guaranteed" is too vague to lower friction.

**Crossroads framing.** Optional but strong. The decision-architecture closer. Pattern: "One path: keep doing what you've been doing. Another path: try this." Frame both paths in the avatar's specific terms. Don't moralize; the protagonist is laying out options, not lecturing.

**PS.** Mandatory. Specific personal detail or callback to story. The PS is the highest-engagement element of every Rosabella variant analyzed; it's where the reader who scrolled to the bottom looking for the offer often gets hooked back into the body. Common patterns: a callback to the antecedent character ("My father called me last week, said his doctor noticed something"); a specific scene from after the decision moment ("First time in 11 years I drove past the pharmacy without stopping in"); a future-pacing line ("This Christmas will be the first one in a decade where I'm not...").

**PPS.** Optional. A second testimonial, an urgency note, or a soft social-proof anchor. The PPS adds a second hook for readers who skipped to the bottom; without it, the PS does the job alone.

### Sufian's 19-Step Crosswalk

The Variant 1 section table maps to Sufian's 19-step framework skeleton (Hook, Lead × 3, Storyline, Identification, Symptoms, Fear, Failed solutions, Real-life consequences, Feelings, Mechanism Problem, Mechanism Solution, Organic Product Intro, Differentiation, Social Proof, Risk Reversal, Urgency, Scarcity, CTA, PS, PPS) as follows:

| Sufian step | Section in this table |
|---|---|
| Hook | Hook + headline |
| Lead × 3 | Three-lead rule (`named-patterns.md` pattern 9): same body, three POV variants |
| Storyline | Antecedent / catalyst story |
| Identification | Identification opener |
| Symptoms | Identification opener (specific sensory + lifestyle markers) |
| Fear | Identification opener (emotional weight beat) |
| Failed solutions | Failed-solution stack |
| Real-life consequences | Antecedent story + identification opener |
| Feelings (Vindication / Loss aversion / etc.) | Five core feelings (`named-patterns.md` pattern 7) |
| Mechanism Problem | Mechanism reveal (Stage 1 + Stage 2 root cause framing) |
| Mechanism Solution | Mechanism reveal (Stage 2 mechanism action + Stage 3) |
| Organic Product Intro | Product introduction |
| Differentiation | Differentiation block + Differentiation vs others (closing) |
| Social Proof | Authority closure + Durability proof + Mirror moment |
| Risk Reversal | Risk reversal |
| Urgency | Crossroads framing |
| Scarcity | Crossroads framing or PPS |
| CTA | CTA (1) + CTA (2) |
| PS | PS |
| PPS | PPS |

Both treatments cover the same architecture. Use Sufian's checklist as a step-by-step writer's checklist; use this section table as the word-budget map. They reinforce each other; pick whichever unit is more useful for the operator workflow.

## Variant 2: Medium Long-Form (1,200 words)

**When to use:** Higher-resistance angles where the user wants a faster read. Solution-aware audiences who have already been pre-educated by a video or upstream ad. Mid-funnel placements where attention is partially captured.

**Word target:** 1,000 to 1,400 words.

**Section rule:** Compress each section by roughly 50%. Do NOT remove sections. The story arc must remain intact. A 1,200-word ad with all sections present outperforms a 1,200-word ad with a complete identification opener but no mechanism.

**Compression guidance per section:**

| Section | Full word budget | Medium word budget | What to cut |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Hook + headline | 30-50 | 30-50 | Don't compress. Hook quality is non-negotiable. |
| Identification opener | 100-200 | 60-100 | Cut secondary symptoms. Keep the core identity marker and one primary symptom. |
| Antecedent / catalyst story | 400-800 | 200-400 | Cut secondary characters. Keep the catalyst and one specific scene. |
| POV anchor + decision moment | 200-400 | 100-200 | Compress to one decision scene with one piece of dialogue. |
| Failed-solution stack | 200-400 | 100-200 | List 2-3 failed solutions instead of 5-7. Specifics still required. |
| Desperation frame + discovery | 300-500 | 150-250 | Compress the buildup. Keep the unusual decision and the discovery itself. |
| Mechanism reveal | 300-500 | 150-250 | Three-stage explanation stays. Compress stage 1 and stage 3; keep the analogy in stage 2 intact. |
| Product introduction | 100-200 | 50-100 | One sentence transition is enough if mechanism is clean. |
| Differentiation block | 200-300 | 100-150 | Two specifics instead of four. |
| Results timeline | 200-400 | 100-200 | Two time-anchored claims instead of four. |
| Authority closure | 100-200 | 0-100 | Optional. First section to drop entirely if budget is tight. |
| Durability proof | 100-200 | 0-100 | Optional. Second section to drop. |
| Mirror moment | 100-200 | 50-100 | Compressed but present. The avatar-mirroring is doing identification work. |
| Differentiation vs others | 200-300 | 100-150 | Two USPs instead of four. |
| Risk reversal | 50-100 | 50-100 | Don't compress. Always present. |
| Crossroads framing | 100-200 | 0-100 | Optional. |
| CTA (1) | 30-50 | 30-50 | Don't compress. |
| PS | 50-100 | 50-100 | Don't compress. The PS is too high-leverage. |
| PPS | 50-100 | 0-50 | Optional. |
| CTA (2) | 30-50 | 0-30 | Optional. |

**Compression rule:** Sections marked "0-X" are the only ones that can be removed. All others must be present even if compressed.

## Variant 3: Fake-Complaint Short-Form (250 words)

**When to use:** ONLY for solution-aware bottom-funnel where the angle has heavy social-proof equity. The brand must have established credibility (testimonials, PR, or repeat customers) for this format to land. Using it for top-of-funnel produces ads that feel hollow and convert poorly.

**Word target:** 200 to 300 words.

**Format:** One paragraph (or two short paragraphs). Customer-to-customer voice. No mechanism. No story arc. No claims about the product.

**Pattern:** Customer complains about something positive. Synthetic abstract beats (illustrative only, not paste-ready copy):

- Sale-price-renewed beat: complaint that an early-buyer discount is still being honored months later, so newer buyers get the same price the writer paid
- Free-shipping-kept beat: complaint that a "promo" free-shipping policy never expired, so the writer's friends got the same deal
- Guarantee-extended beat: complaint that a refund window was honored well past its stated end, so the writer's family bought knowing they had basically no risk

**Why this works:** The complaint frame disarms the reader's filter. They're expecting a sales pitch and instead read a complaint about something positive. The brain processes it as a real customer post, not an ad. Social proof and urgency arrive without claims, which keeps the format compliant in regulated categories.

**Micro-structure:**

| Beat | Word budget | Purpose |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Complaint setup | 30-60 | First-person voice. Identity marker. The "complaint" framing. |
| The positive event | 60-100 | Specifics. What happened. Why it's "annoying." |
| Implicit social proof | 50-100 | Other customers benefiting. Frame as if the writer is annoyed by it. |
| Implicit urgency | 30-60 | "I figured it would end by now." "I'm worried about how long this will last." |
| Soft CTA | 20-40 | "Anyway, here's the link if anyone wants to grab it before they wise up." |

**What to avoid:**

- No mechanism explanation
- No claims about results
- No before/after comparisons
- No mention of the product's specific ingredients or features
- No price claims (the "sale price" is referenced, not quoted)

**Reference:** This format mirrors Rosabella variant #7. See `rosabella-pattern-summary.md` for the structural notes.

## Awareness Stage and Variant Selection Summary

| Awareness stage of angle | Recommended variant | Use case |
|--------------------------|---------------------|----------|
| Problem-unaware | Full long-form | Maximum education runway needed. The reader has to build the entire mental model. |
| Problem-aware | Full long-form | Default. Reader recognizes the problem but has no mechanism to explain why nothing has worked. |
| Solution-aware (high resistance) | Full long-form | Reader has tried multiple solutions. Identification + failed-solution stack must be thorough. |
| Solution-aware (medium resistance) | Medium long-form | Reader is partially primed. Faster read converts better. |
| Solution-aware (heavy social proof) | Fake-complaint short-form | Bottom-funnel only. Customer-to-customer voice, no claims. |
| Product-aware | Medium long-form | Reader already knows the brand. Less education needed. |
| Most-aware (retargeting) | Fake-complaint short-form OR medium long-form | Depends on offer. Short-form for sale-price retargeting, medium for objection-handling. |

**Note on word count vs. line count.** The word ranges above (2,500-3,500 full, 1,000-1,400 medium, 200-300 fake-complaint) are platform-agnostic. Some operators measure in *lines* because Meta primary text formatting (one sentence per line, white-space rules) makes line count the operator's measurable unit in-feed. Approximate conversion at 5-10 words per line of Meta primary text:

- Full long-form: ~500-700 lines (covers the longer end of "Unaware 700-1,000 lines, Problem-Aware 500-800 lines" from operator-side measurement)
- Medium long-form: ~200-280 lines (covers "Solution-Aware 400-600 lines" lower end)
- Fake-complaint short-form: ~40-60 lines

Use whichever unit your operator workflow prefers. The structural rules in this file are unit-agnostic; word budgets and line ranges describe the same body, measured differently.

## Section Length Sanity Checks

After writing, verify total word count and per-section budgets:

```
Full long-form total: 2,500-3,500 words
Medium long-form total: 1,000-1,400 words
Fake-complaint short-form total: 200-300 words
```

If a section exceeds its budget, audit for:

- Repetition (same point made twice in different words)
- Generic filler (sentences that don't advance the arc)
- Over-explanation (mechanism stage 2 should ride on the analogy, not over-detail it)

If a section is under its budget, audit for:

- Missing specifics (named medications, named ages, named durations)
- Missing identification (the avatar's voice is thin)
- Missing bridges (the section ends without connecting to the next)

The deletion pass from `copywriting-guide` applies. Cut any sentence that does not advance the Yes-Yes-Yes chain.
