# Worked Examples

Two long-format static ads broken down beat-by-beat, mapped to the section structure (`section-structure.md`) and named patterns (`named-patterns.md`). Use as audit checklist when reviewing your own draft. If your draft fails a beat that one of these passes, that is the limiting factor.

This file complements `rosabella-pattern-summary.md`. The pattern summary is variant-level meta-analysis (14 Rosabella variants, which patterns each validates). This file is beat-level breakdown (2 ads, what each beat does and why). Different jobs, kept in separate files.

## How to use this file

1. Pick the example whose lead style most closely matches your angle's POV variant (Derila for daughter-discovers-for-parent, Rosabella beetroot for first-person-tells-parent's-story).
2. Read the matching example end-to-end before drafting.
3. After drafting, run the audit checklist at the end of this file against your draft.
4. If your draft fails 3+ checks, return to the example for the specific beat that failed.

The breakdowns reference the existing 10 named patterns by number. Pattern definitions live in `named-patterns.md`; this file does not redefine them.

## Example 1: Derila Ergo (Sleep Pillow)

Twelve-beat breakdown. The ad ran ~3,400 words of primary text; below captures the structural shape, not verbatim copy.

### Beat 1: Pattern Interrupt Hook

**What they wrote (paraphrased, under 15 words):** A daughter's recollection of a 2 AM moment standing in a doorway watching her father struggle to breathe.
**What they did:** Open mid-scene at the worst moment. Identity marker (daughter) inside first 6 words; info gap (what was happening / what changed) implicit.
**Why it works:** Drops the reader inside the situation rather than building up to it. The 2 AM specificity passes the time-anchor test from word one.
**Map:** Hook + headline section in `section-structure.md`. Named-patterns.md pattern 1 (Desperation Frame) opener. Hook quality test from `self-test-checklist.md` item 3.

### Beat 2: Before & Breaking Point (merged)

**What they wrote:** The before-state of her father's life (active, present, the kind of grandfather who wrestled with the kids on the floor) collapsed into a single specific scene where he could not climb a single set of stairs without stopping twice.
**What they did:** Antecedent story compressed by merging "before" and "breaking point" into one beat. The contrast is the agitation engine.
**Why it works:** The reader doesn't need a long backstory. One vivid before-scene plus one collapsed-version scene does the same identification work as four pages of exposition.
**Map:** Identification opener + Antecedent / catalyst story + POV anchor + decision moment, all telescoped into ~400 words rather than ~1,400. Pattern 3 (Identification Before Mechanism) is doing the work; the failed-solution stack hasn't started yet.

### Beat 3: Failed Attempts

**What they wrote:** Itemized list with brands, prices, and durations. CPAP machine ($800, returned after 11 weeks). Mouthguard ($400, kept making him gag). Smart sleep tracker ($200, told him things he already knew). Doctor visits with normal bloodwork.
**What they did:** Specificity. Every failed attempt has a brand or specific descriptor, a dollar amount, a duration. No "I tried everything"; everything is named.
**Why it works:** The reader pattern-matches their own failed attempts. The deeper the specificity, the more matches the reader makes; the more matches, the higher their investment in the protagonist's eventual discovery.
**Map:** Failed-solution stack section. Validates the deep-specificity stack pattern (also seen in Rosabella variants 6, 9, 10).

### Beat 4: The Mechanism (Henrik dialogue)

**What they wrote:** The protagonist's stay at a guesthouse during an unrelated trip. Henrik, the guesthouse owner, asks why she keeps stepping outside to take phone calls in the middle of the night. She explains. Henrik tells her his own father had the same problem. The conversation moves through the mechanism in 4 short exchanges, with Henrik pointing at things (a pillow, a window, a bottle of olive oil) as he explains.
**What they did:** Mechanism reveal as dialogue with informal authority. Henrik is a layperson, not a doctor. The mechanism is delivered as a quote from him, with physical gestures breaking the information density.
**Why it works:** Authority surprise outperforms authority endorsement. A guesthouse owner explaining cardiovascular biology lands differently from a doctor explaining the same thing; the disproportion of the source signals that the information is real and not a sales pitch.
**Map:** Mechanism reveal section. Named-patterns.md pattern 4 (Three-Stage Mechanism Explanation) Stages 1-3, delivered through the pattern 4 dialogue framework subsection (informal authority variant). Pattern 8 (Authority Hook Patterns) "Classic" with informal-authority framing.

### Beat 5: The Discovery

**What they wrote:** The protagonist's reaction to Henrik's explanation. She asks him to repeat one specific phrase three times before it clicks. The relief and recognition arrive in two sentences. She writes the phrase down on a hotel napkin.
**What they did:** The discovery is small, narrow, concrete (a single phrase). Not a general epiphany.
**Why it works:** Specific discoveries land harder than general ones. A phrase the protagonist had to look up multiple times signals real difficulty and earned understanding.
**Map:** Mechanism reveal continuation, transitioning toward product introduction. Named-patterns.md pattern 2 (Discovery Story).

### Beat 6: The Proof Stack

**What they wrote:** Three things that happened in the weeks after the conversation. Her father's snoring stopped on day 4. The sleep tracker she had returned (because it told her things she already knew) was now telling her useful things again. Her mother stopped sleeping in the guest room.
**What they did:** Time-anchored, specific, woven through narrative rather than as a separate "results" section. Each proof point ties to a named relationship or named object from earlier in the body.
**Why it works:** The reader has already invested in the daughter / father / mother dynamic; the proof points land as story progression, not as testimonial.
**Map:** Results timeline, woven through narrative rather than separate. Named-patterns.md pattern 6 (Time-Delay Anchoring).

### Beat 7: Product Reveal

**What they wrote:** A single sentence. The protagonist names the brand and the specific product, in passing, as the product she sourced after Henrik's conversation, after testing several alternatives.
**What they did:** Anticlimactic. The story did the selling; the product reveal is one casual sentence.
**Why it works:** By this point in the body, the reader has accepted the mechanism, recognized themselves in the antecedent, and watched the proof stack arrive. The product is the natural resolution; an aggressive product reveal here would break the spell.
**Map:** Product introduction section. Per the 75% rule (`section-structure.md` Section-Level Writing Notes), the product name appears at approximately 77% of body word count.

### Beat 8: Skepticism & Try

**What they wrote:** A short paragraph naming the protagonist's own initial skepticism. She had returned three sleep products in the past year; she expected to return this one too. She kept the receipt.
**What they did:** Failed-solution stack revisited via humor and self-deprecation. Pre-empts the reader's own "I've been burned before" objection.
**Why it works:** The reader sees their own skepticism reflected. The protagonist's willingness to name her own pattern of returns signals that she isn't blindly endorsing the product.
**Map:** Differentiation block (informal). The "vs the cheap alternatives" work is being done implicitly through the protagonist's previous returns.

### Beat 9: The After

**What they wrote:** A specific scene from 8 weeks later. The father wrestled with the grandkids on the floor for the first time in 4 years. The mother cried. The protagonist describes hanging back to give them the moment.
**What they did:** Mirror moment. Every wound from the identification opener is healed in the After. The "active, present grandfather" image from beat 2 returns, restored.
**Why it works:** The 1:1 mirror between identification and After is the structural payoff of the whole ad. The reader doesn't have to be told the product worked; they see the same image with the wound removed.
**Map:** Durability proof + Mirror moment sections. Named-patterns.md pattern 7 (Five Core Feelings) "identity" feeling sustained from beat 1 through beat 9.

### Beat 10: Offer Stack

**What they wrote:** The protagonist describes that she ordered three more for her father (one for travel, one for the guest room, one as a backup). She mentions the brand offered her a multi-pack discount; she took it.
**What they did:** Differentiation vs others (closing) and risk reversal demonstrated through behavior, not stated. The protagonist's actions are the offer reveal; she's not pitching, she's describing what she did.
**Why it works:** Behavior-based offer reveals are higher-conversion than direct offer pitches. The reader infers the offer's value from the protagonist's choice, not from a sales claim.
**Map:** Differentiation vs others (closing) + Risk reversal sections.

### Beat 11: Urgency & Close

**What they wrote:** A two-sentence aside about the brand running low on inventory; the protagonist had to wait 9 days for her last order. She suggests not waiting.
**What they did:** Urgency framed as a specific past inconvenience (her own 9-day wait). Crossroads framing implicit (she chose to wait; she's recommending the reader skip the wait).
**Why it works:** Specific urgency outperforms generic urgency. "I waited 9 days" is verifiable in the reader's mind; "limited stock" is not.
**Map:** Crossroads framing + CTA section.

### Beat 12: PS

**What they wrote:** A callback to Henrik. The protagonist mentions that Henrik's wife emailed her last week; her own mother had just started sleeping through the night.
**What they did:** PS as a callback to the antecedent character (Henrik), introducing a new but parallel data point (Henrik's wife / mother).
**Why it works:** The PS earns the highest engagement of the body; the reader who scrolled to the bottom for the offer reads the PS and gets pulled back into the story.
**Map:** PS section. Pattern from Rosabella variants 1, 5, 11, 13 (callback-to-story PSs).

### Ten key learnings from the Derila breakdown

1. Open loop intensity at life-or-death level. The hook drops the reader inside the worst moment.
2. Drop the reader INTO the worst moment, do not build up to it.
3. Child witness as nuclear weapon. The daughter's POV multiplies the emotional weight.
4. Failed attempts with exact dollar amounts and the avatar's voice.
5. Mechanism delivered through dialogue with authority, not exposition.
6. Discovery feels accidental (a guesthouse stay during an unrelated trip).
7. Authority surprise outperforms authority endorsement (guesthouse owner, not doctor).
8. Mirror every pain point in the After. The structural payoff is the 1:1 healing.
9. Product reveal in one casual sentence. The story did the selling.
10. Close like a friend giving a tip. "By the way" energy, not "BUY NOW".

## Example 2: Rosabella Beetroot (Cardiovascular Supplement)

Beat-by-beat breakdown from the Rosabella beetroot variant (~2,700 words). Different lead style, different mechanism delivery, same architectural shape.

### Lead style contrast with Derila

- Derila: daughter-discovers-for-parent, third-degree perspective looking inward at family.
- Rosabella beetroot: first-person speaker telling father's story, with the speaker as adult child whose father had two heart attacks before age 67.

The first-degree sufferer is the speaker's father; the speaker is the family-member-of-sufferer (named-patterns.md pattern 9, family-member POV variant). The body anchors in first-person ("I drove out to a farm in rural Pennsylvania") with the father as the off-screen referent.

### Mechanism delivery contrast with Derila

- Derila: dialogue with Henrik, guesthouse owner, urban / European setting, informal authority.
- Rosabella beetroot: dialogue with an Amish farmer, rural Pennsylvania, isolated authority.

Both ads use the pattern 4 dialogue framework (named-patterns.md pattern 4 Delivery Vehicle subsection). Both have the authority character lead the explanation. Both insert physical gestures every 100-150 words (Henrik points at olive oil; the farmer scuffs a boot, holds up a glass, points at a tree). The structural shape is identical; the surface texture differs.

### Alternative attack contrast with Derila

- Derila: attacks CPAP, mouthguards, smart sleep gadgets (the established sleep-aid stack).
- Rosabella beetroot: attacks statin medication specifically (the established cardiovascular pharmaceutical stack).

The alternative attacks are different because the avatars are different, but the pattern is the same: name the specific failed alternatives the avatar has seen or tried, with specificity (statin brand names, doctor names, dosages).

### Same architectural shape

Both ads follow the section structure end-to-end: hook + headline → identification opener → antecedent / catalyst story → POV anchor + decision moment → failed-solution stack → desperation frame + discovery → mechanism reveal (dialogue framework) → product introduction (post-75%) → differentiation block → results timeline → mirror moment → differentiation vs others (closing) → risk reversal → CTA → PS.

## Cross-example patterns

What both ads do:

- Late product reveal (~75-80% mark). Derila at ~77%, Rosabella beetroot at ~78%.
- Mechanism through dialogue with authority character.
- Specific dollar amounts and named brands on failed alternatives.
- Modest, specific results (not dramatic transformations).
- Relational stakes beyond the individual (father / daughter / parents in both).
- Story-as-camouflage all the way through. The ad reads as a personal story end-to-end; the brand surfaces only at the product reveal sentence.

What both ads avoid:

- Marketing language at the product reveal.
- Aggressive tone toward alternatives. CPAP and statins are described as "what didn't work for us", not "the broken industry's failed solutions".
- Stating the mechanism via exposition. The mechanism is always delivered through dialogue.
- Overclaiming. Results are specific and modest; no "miracle" or "life-changing" language.

## Audit Checklist

Run your draft against this checklist. This is INCREMENTAL to the 14-point self-test in `self-test-checklist.md`, not a replacement. The 14-point test still runs first; this checklist runs after, focused on the beats that worked examples have validated.

- **Hook intensity.** Life-or-death level open loop in the first 9 words?
- **Worst-moment opening.** Dropped into the moment, or built up to it?
- **Mechanism delivery.** Dialogue with authority character, or exposition?
- **Failed attempts.** Exact dollar amounts and avatar's voice, or generic?
- **Late reveal.** Product name at or after 75% mark?
- **Mirror in After.** Every identification-opener wound healed in the results timeline?
- **Close tone.** "By the way" energy, or "BUY NOW" energy?

If your draft fails 3+ of these checks, refer back to the example matching your lead variant most closely. Derila for family-discovers-for-sufferer angles; Rosabella beetroot for first-person-tells-parent's-story angles.
